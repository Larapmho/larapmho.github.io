## Effort Estimation and Tracking

Effort estimation became a meaningful and practical part of our ICS 314 final project. Although I had gained some experience estimating time during the practice WODs earlier in the semester, this was my first time formally tracking both coding and non-coding effort throughout a complete application across several milestones. This experience helped me understand my development habits in a more structured and intentional way. One limitation was that my team and I did not track effort during Milestone 1, which was the phase I devoted the most time to, because at that point we were not yet aware that effort tracking would be required. Since most of the features I worked on in later milestones were built on the foundation I created in Milestone 1, a significant portion of my overall work was not recorded and the time I worked on these issues were shorter. Still, the tracking we conducted in Milestones 2 and 3 provided valuable insights into my workflow and use of time.

## How I Made My Effort Estimates

When estimating issues, I drew from prior experience with the WODs, comparisons with earlier tasks, and an assessment of each issue’s complexity. The WODs gave me a sense of how long certain patterns of work, such as building forms, debugging React state, or refining UI layouts, usually required. For example, when estimating the time needed to implement the login system with UH email verification, I relied on similar past exercises that involved validation and error handling. I also compared new issues to previous issues within the project. For example, if I had completed a navigation-related task that took about two hours, I used that as a starting point for estimating similar or slightly more complex tasks. For issues involving unfamiliar logic or edge cases, such as file-size validation or username rules, I added extra time to account for research and potential debugging. At times, our team discussions refined these estimates further, especially for issues with overlapping logic or shared UI components.

## Usefulness of Estimating in Advance

Estimating effort in advance proved beneficial even when the estimates themselves were not perfectly accurate. The act of estimating required me to generally think through each issue step-by-step, which helped clarify the work needed to be done. For instance, I initially believed the message search feature would be quick to complete, but after outlining the necessary tasks, such as search logic, UI integration, and testing, it became clear that it required more time than I assumed. Estimation also helped balance workload within the team by highlighting which issues demanded more concentrated effort. Moreover, the estimates supported more effective communication during milestone planning, allowing us to discuss anticipated workload and adjust responsibilities accordingly.

## Value of Tracking Actual Effort

Tracking actual effort helped me identify consistent patterns in my work. In addition, tracking my effort made me aware of how much time was spent on non-coding work. Activities such as researching documentation, planning approaches, writing out issue details, and discussing design choices with teammates contributed significantly to the project but were not always visible without tracking. Becoming aware of this helped me form a more realistic sense of how my time is distributed during development.

## How I Tracked My Effort

I tracked both coding and non-coding time using a simple timer. Whenever I began working on an issue, I started the timer, and when I stopped, I recorded the elapsed time. I tracked coding and non-coding time separately by noting the category associated with each work session. This method was straightforward and kept me aware of how long various tasks were taking. Manual tracking is never perfectly precise, but I believe the approach provided a reasonably accurate representation of my effort.

## Reflections and Future Improvements

Looking back, I would adjust several parts of my process. I would include more explicit buffers for verification, debugging, and communication, because those activities consistently required more time than I anticipated. I would also break larger issues into smaller ones to create more accurate estimates and clearer milestones. In the future, I would consider using a more structured approach to tracking non-coding tasks to increase accuracy. I also plan to integrate AI more intentionally into my workflow by estimating the effort spent on prompt creation, verification, and integration separately, rather than treating AI assistance as uniformly time-saving.
AI Use for Estimation and Tracking

I used ChatGPT for clarifying unfamiliar concepts, breaking tasks into logical steps, and interpreting confusing behavior in my code.Although AI suggestions were helpful for planning and problem-solving, they still required careful review, editing, and testing before being used. I relied on AI for conceptual clarity and idea generation rather than as a replacement for my own implementation work. Because AI-generated code requires adaptation and verification, I view AI as a supplemental tool rather than something that reduces total effort in a straightforward way.

## Conclusion

Effort estimation gave me a clearer understanding of how I work, how long different tasks require, and how important it is to separate coding from non-coding activities. Even though we did not track Milestone 1 and therefore missed capturing the phase where I invested the most effort, the tracking completed during Milestones 2 and 3 still allowed me to see patterns in my workflow and make more realistic estimates. Using a simple timer and intentionally recording each work session helped me develop a more accurate picture of how my time is distributed. This experience taught me the value of building buffers into estimates, breaking issues into smaller tasks, improving non-coding time tracking, and integrating AI tools in a more thoughtful and transparent way. I plan to continue refining these practices in future software projects to improve accuracy, planning, and overall project management.
